Product Strategy · 2026

Drinking Democracy

Market-Leader Product Roadmap for a distinctive, replayable and responsible social party game
Do not build another generic deck of drinking prompts. Build the party game where democracy itself creates the chaos.
Prepared 1 August 2026 · Based on the current Expo application, prompt system, Virus mechanics, personalization flow and competitive market

Executive recommendation

Drinking Democracy already has a strong foundation: rapid local setup, personalized player names, themed prompt packs, multilingual presentation, mixed sessions, offline fallback content and paired Virus rules. The opportunity is to evolve it from a randomized prompt deck into a recognizable game system with elections, persistent laws, power shifts, callbacks and a satisfying finale.

Winning position: the first party game in which players elect leaders, pass ridiculous laws, form coalitions, expose scandals and eventually bring the government crashing down.

Competitors already compete on content volume and breadth. Picolo demonstrates the reach of simple name-driven, one-device play. Drinkin’ advertises numerous formats, custom prompts, timers and roulette. Exposed and Nightly advertise room codes, remote play and thousands of prompts. Duckz advertises touchscreen minigames, custom lists and offline play. Drinking Democracy should meet the best table stakes, but win through a stronger core loop and clearer identity.

1. The signature Democracy mode

This should become the flagship experience and the product’s defensible identity. A session should feel like a short story rather than a shuffled list.

Act I · The Campaign

Icebreakers, nominations, campaign speeches, quick votes and the first election.

Act II · The Government

Officeholders gain powers, the group passes Laws, coalitions form and players earn influence.

Act III · The Collapse

Scandals, betrayals, confidence votes, snap elections and escalating callbacks.

The Finale

A final referendum determines the winner from influence and decisions—not alcohol consumed.

Core mechanics

  • Elect a President, Taoiseach, Minister for Chaos, Party Whip or similarly playful office.
  • Hold an election every six to eight cards, with pacing adapted to session length.
  • Award influence for votes, challenges, successful predictions and completed objectives.
  • Let players form temporary coalitions and break them when incentives change.
  • Give officeholders one-use powers such as veto, immunity, recount, appointment or pardon.
  • Trigger scandals that weaken or remove an officeholder.
  • End with a confidence vote or final referendum and a clear winner.

2. Evolve Virus cards into Laws

Virus cards are the most distinctive mechanic already in the application. They should become visible, persistent and interactive.

  • Rename Virus cards to Laws within Democracy mode while retaining legacy category support internally.
  • Display every active Law in a persistent tray accessible from the card screen.
  • Show who introduced each Law and how long it has survived.
  • Remind the group when a Law is relevant instead of relying on memory alone.
  • Allow repeal, amendment, transfer and conflicting Laws that require a vote.
  • Support global Laws, player-specific Laws, role Laws and team Laws.
  • Include “longest surviving Law” and “most Laws passed” in the recap.
Example Laws: “All speeches must begin with ‘My fellow citizens…’” · “Nobody may use the word ‘drink’ until this Law is repealed.” · “Anyone who points must nominate a new Minister.”

3. Separate format from tone

Prinks, Messy and Flirty should become tones that can be layered over multiple game formats rather than functioning as the entire mode definition.

The mix screen should support weighted percentages—such as 50% Prinks, 30% Messy and 20% Flirty—plus toggles for personal questions, physical challenges, spicy content, public-place suitability and required props.

4. New modes with real mechanical differences

Coalition Chaos

Teams negotiate, share objectives, compete for influence and can betray one another.

Secret Ballot

Players vote privately using pass-the-phone screens or optional connected devices.

Five-Second Filibuster

Give three answers before the timer expires.

Scandal

Answer, bluff or challenge another player’s story.

Impostor in Government

One hidden player secretly sabotages group challenges.

Cabinet Shuffle

Every player receives a role with a limited special ability.

Referendum

Rapid “would you rather” and “most likely to” voting rounds.

Last Orders

A high-energy ten-minute finale for the end of a party.

Date Night

A properly balanced two-player experience rather than a reduced group deck.

Mocktail Mode

All penalties become points, dares or water, widening the addressable audience.

Pub Quiz

Irish culture, music, local trivia and customizable group knowledge.

Public Mode

Removes shouting, movement and stranger-interaction prompts when playing at a venue.

5. Build a session director

Replace pure random selection with a deliberate pacing engine.

Icebreakers → Personalization → Competition → Chaos → Callback → Finale
  • Target every player fairly and avoid repeatedly selecting the same person.
  • Adapt prompts to player count, selected relationships, venue and boundaries.
  • Avoid consecutive cards with the same structure or emotional intensity.
  • Introduce Laws early enough to affect the game and end them deliberately.
  • Increase intensity gradually rather than allowing an extreme first card.
  • Remember earlier votes and answers to generate callbacks later in the session.
  • Reserve a strong final sequence instead of ending when the array is exhausted.
High-value callback: “Earlier, everyone voted that Alex was most likely to become famous. Alex must now deliver their acceptance speech.” These callbacks make the game appear attentive and create stories unique to each group.

6. Fast touchscreen minigames

Minigames should last less than twenty seconds and raise the room’s energy without turning the app into a collection of unrelated arcade games.

  • Everyone places a finger on the screen; one player is selected.
  • Reaction-time duel and rapid-tap campaign.
  • Hold-the-button bomb timer.
  • Spin the policy wheel.
  • Five-second answer timer.
  • Dice, coin flip and random team generator.
  • Pass-the-phone secret mission.
  • Two-team buzzer and quick prediction rounds.
  • “Do not laugh” timer without recording or retaining video.

7. Deeper player personalization

  • Let players reroll or choose the new themed avatar assigned to them.
  • Support optional pronouns, nicknames and lightweight role preferences.
  • Remember frequent groups locally for one-tap setup.
  • Optionally identify couples or relationships to improve prompt suitability.
  • Support mobility and accessibility restrictions.
  • Track fair targeting during a session.
  • Attach temporary offices, powers, teams and influence to player avatars.
  • Allow late joining and graceful player removal without restarting.

Do not create daily drinking streaks or permanent consumption leaderboards. Progression should reward game variety, hosting and fun—not alcohol frequency.

8. Make customization genuinely powerful

Private custom cards

Players should be able to create, edit, reorder, disable and test custom cards stored only on their device. This is faster and more useful than the current email-only contribution flow.

Community packs

A later release could share packs through a short code or QR code. Public community content must include filtering, reporting, moderation, age controls and published contact information.

Prompt metadata

  • Card type, tone and intensity.
  • Minimum and maximum player count.
  • Number of targeted players.
  • Venue suitability and required props.
  • Physical-contact and sensitive-topic flags.
  • Estimated duration, cooldown and follow-up card identifiers.
  • Language, human-review status and translation completeness.

9. Safety as product quality

Responsible design expands the audience, improves trust and protects store eligibility. Apple’s guidelines prohibit apps that encourage excessive alcohol consumption; a disclaimer alone is insufficient.

  • Confirm legal age appropriate to the player’s region.
  • Let the group choose sips, points, water or dares as the consequence system.
  • Never require shots, chugging or fixed quantities of alcohol.
  • Provide a clearly visible, no-penalty skip control.
  • Collect consent and physical-contact boundaries during setup.
  • Insert occasional water, food and pause cards.
  • Display a “do not drink and drive” reminder at the end.
  • Remove dangerous, illegal, humiliating or coercive challenges.
  • Allow any player to privately disable spicy, personal or physical content.
  • Never punish someone for declining a card.

10. Moment-to-moment polish

  • Haptic feedback for votes, reveals, elections and major events.
  • Short optional sounds for scandals, victories and repealed Laws.
  • Category-specific card entrances and restrained celebratory animation.
  • Visible progress through the current act and estimated time remaining.
  • A pause screen showing active Laws, roles and session settings.
  • First-session gesture hints for tapping backward and forward.
  • Keep the display awake during a live session.
  • Sound, haptics, large-text and reduced-motion settings.
  • Landscape or tabletop display mode.
  • Resume an interrupted game and restart using the most recent group.
  • No advertisements or paywall interruptions during active play.

11. A rewarding Game Over experience

Reaching the end should produce a payoff and an immediate reason to play again.

  • Winner or elected Supreme Leader.
  • Most targeted player and most election victories.
  • Longest-surviving Law and most Laws passed.
  • Biggest scandal and most frequently nominated player.
  • Successful group challenges and coalition history.
  • A generated name for the group’s government.
  • Rematch with the same settings or save the current mix as a preset.
  • An optional shareable graphic containing nicknames and harmless statistics only.

12. Content and live-operations strategy

  • Fresh monthly prompt drops and visible pack versioning.
  • Seasonal packs for Halloween, Christmas, festivals, Eurovision and graduation.
  • Irish Night and regional culture packs.
  • Birthday, hen/stag, housewarming and university packs.
  • Human-reviewed Irish, Polish and Spanish prompt translations.
  • Post-game thumbs-up/down feedback rather than mid-game interruptions.
  • “Never show this card again” and session-level skip reasons.
  • Duplicate and near-duplicate detection in the content pipeline.
  • Use skip and dislike rates to prioritize editorial improvements.

The quality target should be strong setup, fairness and callbacks—not an inflated prompt-count claim.

13. Trustworthy monetization

  • Keep a complete, replayable Democracy experience free.
  • Sell optional themed packs or an honest one-time complete-pack unlock.
  • Add a seasonal membership only when the release cadence genuinely supports it.
  • Avoid weekly-trial dark patterns and clearly explain renewal terms.
  • Do not display advertisements between cards.
  • Clearly state what remains available after cancellation.
  • Ensure purchased packs remain playable offline.

Recommended success metrics

  • Median time from launch to first card under fifteen seconds.
  • Session completion and rematch rates by selected duration.
  • Thirty-day “next party” return rate rather than daily-use pressure.
  • Per-card skip, dislike and report rates.
  • Fairness variance: how evenly players are targeted in each session.
  • Percentage of sessions using Laws, custom mixes and the finale.
  • Crash-free sessions and successful offline starts.
The single most important decision: turn persistent Laws, elections and political chaos into the core loop. Competitors can add more prompts; they cannot easily copy a coherent identity, progression system and content engine built around democracy.
